The field guide

Six species. Six different problems.

Explorers arrive able to name these animals. They leave able to explain what each one needs to survive, and why that is harder for some than others. Conservation status follows the IUCN Red List.

Near Threatened

Southern White Rhino

Ceratotherium simum simum

Poaching driven by a belief that rhino horn has medicinal value. It does not: horn is keratin, the same protein as fingernails and hair. Southern white rhino numbers recovered from fewer than 100 animals to roughly 20,000, one of conservation’s genuine successes, and were then cut back hard by a sustained poaching wave. South Africa holds most of the world’s population, so what happens in reserves like this one decides the subspecies.

In the fieldThe realities of anti-poaching work, and the reasoning behind dehorning — why a reserve would remove the thing it is trying to protect.

Endangered

African Savanna Elephant

Loxodonta africana

A keystone species: it pushes over trees, digs for water in dry riverbeds and disperses seed across huge distances, physically building the habitat that other species then depend on. Its problem is space. Elephants need connected range, and range is exactly what fencing, farmland and settlement remove. Listed as Endangered in 2021 after decades of decline.

In the fieldReading feeding damage and dung to work out herd size, composition and how long ago they moved through.

Vulnerable

Cheetah

Acinonyx jubatus

Built for one thing: a sprint that is over in around thirty seconds. That specialisation is expensive. A cheetah that makes a kill frequently loses it to lions or hyenas, it needs vast open ground to hunt at all, and the species carries unusually low genetic diversity. Only a few thousand mature adults remain across the whole continent.

In the fieldCheetah feeding and monitoring sessions, and why individual animals are tracked by name and identification record rather than counted in bulk.

Vulnerable

Leopard

Panthera pardus

The most adaptable big cat in Africa and by some distance the hardest to count. Solitary, largely nocturnal, and entirely capable of living close to people without ever being seen. That adaptability disguises a steady contraction of range, and it is why leopards are surveyed by camera trap rather than by eye.

In the fieldSetting and checking camera traps, then working through the captures the way a researcher does, including everything that turns out to be a bush moving.

Vulnerable

African Lion

Panthera leo

Africa’s only truly social cat, and the species that states human-wildlife conflict in its sharpest form: a predator that occasionally kills livestock, living beside people who depend on that livestock to eat. Lion range has collapsed across most of the continent and the great majority of the remaining population now survives inside protected areas.

In the fieldNight drives, listening for contact calls, and a direct conversation about what coexistence costs the people who carry it.

Least Concern

African Fish Eagle

Icthyophaga vocifer

Not everything on the list is in trouble, and that matters. As a top predator on healthy water systems, this bird is an indicator species: where it is calling, hunting and breeding, the wetland underneath it is usually functioning. Learning to read what is common is as useful as learning to recognise what is rare.

In the fieldIdentification by call and silhouette, then using indicator species to assess habitat health rather than just to tick a list.

Why Dinokeng

An unusually honest classroom.

Dinokeng is not untouched wilderness, and that is the point. Neighbouring landowners in Gauteng took down the fences between their properties and reintroduced free-roaming Big Five game to a landscape that had been farmed for generations. It is a rewilding story with a start date.

The reserve sits about an hour from OR Tambo International Airport and is malaria-free, which removes both a long internal transfer and a prophylaxis conversation from the trip.

More usefully for a teenager, it shows conservation as something people decided to build and now have to actively manage: fences, water, disease, genetics, budgets, neighbours. A wilderness that appears to look after itself teaches nobody anything. This one cannot pretend.

What conservation is up against

Three problems with no easy answer.

We do not hand explorers a simplified version. These are the pressures the reserve manages every week, and the trade-offs are genuinely difficult.

Pressure 01

Poaching and the illegal wildlife trade

Rhino horn is keratin and medicinally worthless, yet it commands extraordinary prices because enough people believe otherwise. Protecting a single animal means patrols, tracking dogs, night surveillance, and sometimes removing the horn to make that animal worthless to a poacher. Explorers hear how those decisions get made and what they cost.

Pressure 02

Habitat loss and fragmentation

Animals do not need land, they need connected land. A reserve cut off from its neighbours becomes a genetic island, and island populations lose diversity and the room to move through drought. The remedy is unglamorous and slow: clearing invasive species, replanting indigenous trees, repairing eroded ground.

Pressure 03

Human-wildlife coexistence

An elephant that flattens a season’s crops overnight is a conservation success to a visitor and a financial disaster to the family that planted them. Conservation only holds where the people living alongside wildlife see a genuine benefit in carrying that cost. Explorers meet the argument from both sides before deciding what they think.

Fresh spoor. Tracking is the first skill taught and the last one mastered.
